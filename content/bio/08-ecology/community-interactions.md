### Species interactions, by who wins

| Interaction | Species A | Species B | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Competition** | − | − | Two barnacles on one rock |
| **Predation** | **+** | − | Otter and urchin |
| **Parasitism** | **+** | − | Tapeworm and host |
| **Mutualism** | **+** | **+** | Bee and flower |
| **Commensalism** | **+** | 0 | Bird nesting in a tree |

Predation and parasitism are both **+/−**. The difference: a **predator kills
and eats** its prey, usually quickly. A **parasite keeps its host alive** —
because a dead host is a dead parasite. That is why parasites tend to evolve
toward being **less** lethal over time.

True commensalism is **rare on close inspection** — look hard enough and some
small effect usually turns up.

### Niches: what you could do vs what you actually do

- **Fundamental niche** — the full range of conditions a species *could* use if
  nothing else were present
- **Realised niche** — the narrower range it **actually** occupies, once
  competitors and predators are accounted for

**Competitive exclusion:** two species cannot occupy exactly the same niche in
the same place indefinitely. One will displace the other.

But the usual outcome is **not extinction** — it is **resource partitioning**.
Each is pushed into the part of the range where it wins, and both persist in
narrower niches. This is a major reason communities contain so many
similar-looking species that are not actually competing head-on.

### Keystone species

**Sea otters** eat urchins; urchins eat kelp. Remove the otters and the urchins
explode, strip the kelp, and the entire kelp forest — habitat for hundreds of
species — collapses.

> A **keystone species** has influence **far out of proportion to its
> abundance**. Named for the stone that holds an arch up.

Also: wolves in Yellowstone, starfish on rocky shores.

**The practical lesson: you cannot judge a species' importance by counting it.**
Conservation decisions based on abundance alone can remove the thing holding
the structure together.

### Succession

| | **Primary** | **Secondary** |
|---|---|---|
| Starts from | **Bare rock, no soil** | Disturbed ground, **soil intact** |
| Caused by | Volcanic eruption, retreating glacier | Fire, flood, abandoned farmland |
| Pioneers | Lichens, mosses | Fast-growing weeds and grasses |
| Speed | **Centuries** | **Decades** |

**Soil is the bottleneck.** Primary succession has to *build* soil from rock,
which takes an extremely long time. Secondary succession inherits soil already
full of nutrients, seeds, fungi and bacteria — the hard part is already done.

> Which is exactly why **soil erosion** is so serious: slow to build, quick to
> lose.

### Eutrophication — harm two steps downstream

Fertiliser runoff enters a lake. Then:

1. Nitrogen and phosphorus normally **limit** algal growth — the limit is removed
2. **Algae bloom** enormously
3. The bloom dies
4. **Decomposer bacteria** feast and multiply
5. Their **respiration strips the oxygen** from the water
6. **Fish suffocate** — a dead zone

Notice the shape: adding nutrients looks harmless, and **the thing that kills
the fish is the decomposers, not the fertiliser.** Ecosystem interventions have
consequences that are indirect and delayed, which is what makes them so hard to
manage.

### Why invasive species explode

At home, a species sits near its carrying capacity, held there by a web of
**density-dependent factors** — predators, parasites, diseases, competitors —
that **coevolved with it over a very long time**.

Move it somewhere new and **almost none of them come too**. The brakes are
gone, and the population grows exponentially, exactly as any population does
when its limiting factors are removed. Meanwhile native species have **no
evolved defences** against it.

This is **enemy release**, and it explains rabbits in Australia, cane toads,
zebra mussels and Japanese knotweed.

It also explains why **biological control** is both attempted and risky:
introducing a predator to control an invader can create a second invasion.
